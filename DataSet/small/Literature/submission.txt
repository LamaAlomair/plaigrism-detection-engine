The intense suffering of this experience left a deep and permanent mark on Dostoevsky’s mind. Although his religious nature eventually led him to accept every suffering with resignation, and even to view it as a blessing in his own case, he constantly returned to this subject throughout his writings. He describes the awful agony of the condemned man and insists on the cruelty involved in inflicting such torment upon a person. After his near-execution, he spent four years in penal servitude among common criminals in Siberia, where he began writing what later became the “Dead House,” followed by several years of duty in a disciplinary battalion.

Before his arrest, he had already shown signs of an obscure nervous condition, and this now developed into violent attacks of epilepsy, a disorder that plagued him for the remainder of his life. The fits came several times a year, often increasing during moments of great emotional strain. In 1859, he received permission to return to Russia. Soon after, he founded a journal, “Vremya,” which was forbidden by the Censorship through a misunderstanding, halting the project unexpectedly. In 1864, he suffered the loss of both his first wife and his brother Mihail, events that added further sorrow to an already troubled period in his life.
