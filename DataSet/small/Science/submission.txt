The acids, for example, are compounded of two substances, of the order of those which we consider as simple, and one of these always constitutes acidity and is common to all acids, so that from this substance the name of the genus or class ought properly to be taken. The other substance, however, is peculiar to each acid, and distinguishes it from the rest, and from this the specific name is derived. But, in the greatest number of acids, the two constituent elements, the acidifying principle, and that which it acidifies, are capable of existing in different proportions, producing every possible state of equilibrium or saturation. This situation is exactly the case in the sulphuric and the sulphurous acids, and we have chosen to mark these two states of the same acid by varying the termination of the specific name so that their differences may be clearly expressed.

Metallic substances that have been exposed to the joint action of the air and of fire, like the acids, lose their metallic lustre, increase in weight, and take on an earthy appearance. In such a state, they too are compounded of a principle which is common to all, together with one that is peculiar to each metal. In the same manner, therefore, we determined it was proper to class them under a generic name, derived from the common principle, for which reason we adopted the term oxyd, and then we distinguish them from each other by the particular name of the metal to which each of these substances belongs.
